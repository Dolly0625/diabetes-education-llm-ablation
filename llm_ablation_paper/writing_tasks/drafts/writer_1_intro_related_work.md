# 1. Introduction

Diabetes education chatbots are increasingly deployed to support long-term self-management among patients with chronic conditions, many of whom are elderly with limited health literacy [@Sng2023-diabetes-pitfalls]. While these systems can deliver consistent, accessible guidance on diet, medication adherence, and blood glucose monitoring, their true safety risks rarely manifest during routine educational interactions. Instead, the critical vulnerabilities emerge when patients persistently pressure the assistant—demanding self-adjustment of insulin dosages, requesting online diagnoses, seeking endorsement of unverified folk remedies, or presenting acute hypoglycemic symptoms that require immediate emergency referral [@Huang2023-diabetes-accuracy]. Prior evaluations of diabetes chatbots have identified specific failure modes including confusion between mg/dL and mmol/L units, rigid dietary recommendations, and inability to recognize pseudohypoglycemia [@Sng2023-diabetes-pitfalls], underscoring that adversarial pressure—not routine conversation—constitutes the most informative test of safety boundaries.

However, relying solely on system prompts to enforce safety constraints is demonstrably insufficient. Safety-aligned prompts can be circumvented through multi-turn pressure, jailbreak techniques, or persistent rephrasing of harmful requests [@MedSafetyBench2024]. MedSafetyBench demonstrated that even models with safety-tuned prompts remain vulnerable to gradient-based adversarial attacks in medical contexts [@MedSafetyBench2024], while WildGuard showed that distinguishing genuine harmfulness from appropriate refusal requires dedicated moderation architectures rather than prompt-level instructions alone [@WildGuard2024]. These findings motivate a shift toward system-level, programmatic, and auditable safeguards—layered controls that operate at the architectural level rather than relying exclusively on the language model's internalized alignment.

Yet existing evaluations of medical LLM safety predominantly assess systems under a single, holistic configuration, reporting aggregate safety metrics without attributing outcomes to individual architectural components [@HealthBench2025; @AgentClinic2026]. HealthBench evaluates models as monolithic black boxes across 5,000 multi-turn health conversations [@HealthBench2025], and AgentClinic benchmarks multi-agent clinical workflows without isolating the contribution of specific safety layers [@AgentClinic2026]. This black-box evaluation paradigm leaves a fundamental question unanswered: when a system comprises multiple layered controls—a dialogue planner, dynamic tool gating, and output guardrails—which layer changes which type of error, and at what cost?

To address this layer-attribution gap, we present an exploratory, non-preregistered adversarial stress test of a diabetes education LLM assistant, evaluating four incrementally layered configurations under a strict unique-difference principle ({FIG:1}): **Base (A)**, **Planner (B)**, **Planner + Gate (C)**, and **Full-stack (D)**. Notably, Base (A) incorporates a globally active Input Guard against prompt injections and self-harm crises (identical across all four configurations) and is therefore not an unmoderated prompt-only baseline. In this simulated, non-clinical evaluation using a designated model and version, we pose four research questions:

- **RQ1 (Critical Safety Failure):** Does the incremental addition of a dialogue planner, dynamic tool gating, and an output guard alter the observed critical failure rate (CFR) across conditions Base (A) through Full-stack (D) under adversarial scripted scenarios?
- **RQ2 (Factual and State Consistency):** How do the layered controls affect factual and state consistency, including the fabrication of unsupported negative patient history from conversational silence?
- **RQ3 (Over-Refusal):** Do the progressively stricter layered controls induce over-refusal—inappropriately declining to answer benign diabetes education queries?
- **RQ4 (Scanner–Judge Agreement):** What is the level of agreement between deterministic rule-based scanners and blinded LLM judges, and what are the directional patterns of their disagreement? (The scanner is not treated as ground truth.)

We tested 23 adversarial cases across four conditions, generating **92** complete trajectories with **204** assistant turns and **0** exclusions (**23/23** complete paired blocks), evaluated by a blinded LLM judge at a total cost of **US$0.8903688 ≈ TWD 28.49** (source: `../safety_stress_test/v2/V2_FULL_METRICS.json`). As an exploratory, non-preregistered study conducted in a simulated environment with a designated model version and LLM judge scoring, our central finding is not that any single configuration is safest, but that layered defenses redistribute errors in non-monotonic, context-dependent patterns—a phenomenon we term *error redistribution*.

# 2. Related Work

## 2.1 Medical Dialogue LLMs and Simulated Patient Evaluation

Multi-turn medical dialogue evaluation has increasingly adopted simulated patient paradigms to assess clinical reasoning under realistic conversational conditions. AMIE demonstrated that an LLM system trained via self-play and chain-of-reasoning can match or exceed primary care physicians across 32 clinical communication axes in a randomized, double-blind crossover OSCE with 20 standardized patient actors and 20 physicians [@AMIE2025]. Multimodal AMIE extended this paradigm to incorporate real-time image and document interpretation within diagnostic conversations, introducing explicit hallucination severity grading across 105 scenarios evaluated by 18 specialists [@MultimodalAMIE2026]. AgentClinic constructed a four-agent benchmark (Doctor, Patient, Measurement, and Moderator LLMs) for sequential clinical decision-making under incomplete information, supporting up to 20 turns and evaluating robustness across 23 cognitive bias conditions [@AgentClinic2026]. CRAFT-MD revealed that when 2,000 static medical exam questions are converted into dynamic multi-turn dialogues, model accuracy degrades substantially, exposing limitations in active history-taking and cross-turn information integration [@CRAFT-MD2025].

These systems have advanced the evaluation of conversational diagnostic quality. However, their primary focus remains on diagnostic accuracy and communication effectiveness; few subject the system to sustained adversarial safety pressure—the scenario most critical for patient-facing diabetes education chatbots operating under persistent user demands.

## 2.2 Medical Safety Benchmarks and Adversarial Stress Testing

Medical safety benchmarks have begun to incorporate adversarial and red-teaming mechanisms, though most remain limited to single-turn or general-health contexts. HealthBench assembled 5,000 multi-turn health conversations with 48,562 physician-authored rubrics spanning 262 clinicians across 60 countries, including adversarial red-teaming scenarios and worst-of-*n* reliability analysis [@HealthBench2025]. MedSafetyBench grounded its 1,800 medical safety test cases in the AMA Code of Medical Ethics, employing Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) adversarial attacks to generate 900 medical jailbreak prompts validated by 25 physicians [@MedSafetyBench2024]. WildGuard introduced a unified open-source moderation tool trained on 92,000 real and adversarial synthetic examples, critically distinguishing between response harmfulness and refusal behavior—a distinction directly relevant to the over-refusal metric (RQ3) in our study [@WildGuard2024]. AgentHarm extended safety evaluation to agentic settings, measuring harmful task completion rates across multi-step, dependency-laden tool-calling sequences spanning 11 harm categories [@AgentHarm2025].

These benchmarks have established harm taxonomies and adversarial evaluation methodologies. However, they predominantly test models as monolithic black boxes; the independent contribution of individual safety layers—planner, tool gate, output guard—is rarely measured in isolation.

## 2.3 Layered Programmatic Safeguards and Ablation Studies

The transition from prompt-based to programmatic safety controls has motivated research on decomposable, layer-wise safety architectures. ToolEmu used LLM-emulated sandbox environments to evaluate agent behavior under underspecified user instructions, conducting explicit ablation experiments on safety constraint prompts and quantifying the trade-off between risk reduction and task completion [@ToolEmu2024]. MedAgentBench tested 12 LLM agents executing 300 clinical tasks against real FHIR API electronic health records, employing purely rule-based graders to verify API payload correctness—demonstrating the viability of programmatic verification over free-text evaluation for clinical action safety [@MedAgentBench2025]. In the diabetes education domain specifically, RISE proposed a retrieval-augmented framework incorporating 24 safety rules and two-step fact-checking, evaluated by both clinicians and patients, providing an early example of layered guardrail design for diabetes self-management [@RISE2024].

Existing guardrail studies, however, typically evaluate a single mechanism or compare full-system configurations without strict ablation control. Our study applies a unique-difference principle—toggling exactly one component between adjacent conditions (A→B: planner; B→C: dynamic tool gate; C→D: output guard)—to enable layer-specific attribution of error changes.

## 2.4 LLM-as-a-Judge: Scalability, Biases, and Clinical Gaps

The use of LLMs as automated evaluators offers scalability and cost advantages but introduces systematic biases requiring careful consideration. The foundational MT-Bench study established that strong LLM judges can achieve >80% agreement with human expert preferences, while simultaneously identifying three inherent biases: position bias, verbosity bias, and self-enhancement bias [@MTBench2023]. FairEval further quantified position bias, reporting conflict rates (judgment reversal upon order swapping) as high as 46.3% for GPT-4 [@FairEval2024].

In clinical domains, the gap between LLM judges and human clinicians becomes especially consequential. Croxford et al. found that the best-performing LLM judge achieved an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.818 with physician panels on clinical summary quality—notably approaching the inter-clinician agreement ceiling rather than demonstrating independent correctness [@PDSQI2025]. MedJUDGE's scoping review of 49 medical LLM-as-a-judge studies revealed that 73.5% relied exclusively on GPT-family models and 26.5% involved no human expert validation whatsoever, warning of shared blind spots across model lineages [@MedJUDGE2026]. Most strikingly, DeLucia et al. demonstrated that even when LLM judges and clinicians reach the same verdict, their underlying clinical reasoning aligns in only 24.6% of cases, with judge discriminative AUC ranging from 0.49 to 0.66—near chance level [@SameVerdict2026].

These findings underscore that LLM judges, including the blinded same-model repeated evaluation employed in our study, are subject to shared-model bias and should not be equated with human clinical review. We adopt this evaluation approach for its reproducibility and low cost while explicitly acknowledging these methodological limitations.

## 2.5 Research Gap

Existing evaluations of medical dialogue LLMs rarely attribute safety changes to individual layered controls under a fixed model. Prior studies have seldom simultaneously measured critical safety failure rate, factual-state consistency, over-refusal, and scanner–judge disagreement within a single diabetes education system under strict ablation control. This exploratory study addresses this gap by applying a unique-difference design across four incrementally layered configurations, evaluated under adversarial pressure scenarios specific to diabetes self-management.
